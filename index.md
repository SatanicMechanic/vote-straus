## The Cascade

This is not a campaign that promises to manage the government more efficiently. It is built on the premise that what the Oregon state government does is illegitimate, counterproductive, or both, a position supported by decades of evidence, billions of misspent dollars, and the exposed track record of the people currently in charge.

The positions here follow from first principles: you own yourself and the fruits of your labor, voluntary exchange beats political allocation, and the power of government is a danger to everyone it claims to serve — especially those it most loudly claims to protect.

Portland's signature crises — homelessness, drug addiction, property crime, mental illness, unaffordable housing — are treated as separate problems requiring separate agencies and separate budgets. They are not separate. They are one system, and every link in the chain has a government intervention at its root.

## Housing & Homelessness

**Position: Let people build. Every lot line, height cap, and design review is a law that made housing scarcer on purpose; repeal them.**

Portland has a severe housing shortage. The standard political diagnosis, that the market has failed, gets the causality backwards. The market has been prevented from functioning. Oregon's statewide land use planning system (ORS Chapter 197), urban growth boundaries, and local zoning codes collectively function as a cartel protecting existing property owners from competition. Every minimum lot size, maximum height limit, setback requirement, and conditional use process raises the cost of adding housing.

**Positions:**
- Repeal Oregon's statewide land use planning statutes. The Urban Growth Boundary is an anti-competitive moat that restricts the land available for development and insulates existing property owners from competition. Portland solved sprawl the way a tourniquet solves circulation.
- Eliminate zoning restrictions on density, use, and building height at the state level, and preempt local governments from reimposing them.
- End all state-mandated permitting delays and design review requirements that add months and tens of thousands of dollars to housing construction.
- Repeal rent control statutes. Rent control destroys supply and benefits current tenants at the expense of everyone else seeking housing. The economic evidence on this is overwhelming and one-sided.

[Read more: Rent Control's Winners and Losers — Stanford GSB](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/insights/rent-controls-winners-losers)

- End state and local government ownership of housing. Government housing allocates units politically rather than by need and insulates itself from quality competition.

Add the appropriations together — agency budgets, one-time packages, emergency declarations — and state housing and homelessness spending since 2018 passes $4.5 billion.[^spend] Over the last two years of it, the population being served grew by more than a third. The count itself deserves skepticism in both directions: it rises when a county improves its data collection and falls when the state adds shelter beds, and neither is the same thing as fewer people without homes. Oregon added more than 3,000 beds and still counted 16,512 people unsheltered.[^pit] Oregon has since become one of the only states in the country to exempt the financial details of subsidized housing projects from its public records law, so the question of where the money went is now, by statute, harder to ask.

The visible homelessness crisis in Portland is inseparable from drug addiction and mental illness. Government has not produced effective treatment for either at scale, and its "services" often function as an incentive structure that keeps people in crisis rather than helping them exit it. Voluntary private charity, mutual aid, and faith-based recovery operate at lower cost and without the perverse incentives that plague government-funded programs.

The reason the crisis is visible on every block is government ownership. When the sidewalk in front of your business belongs to the city, you have no standing to maintain it, and when the city refuses to, no one can. You are taxed for the upkeep of property you do not control, then told to be compassionate about its deterioration. Private ownership solves this: an owner has both the authority and the incentive to act. Government ownership guarantees that no one does.

## Drug Policy

**Position: Legalize the supply. Decriminalizing possession while criminalizing the market is not a policy — it is a subsidy for cartels.**

What a person puts into their own body is their business, not the government's. The drug war has been one of the most destructive government programs in modern history: it militarized police, filled prisons disproportionately with poor and minority defendants, created the cartels, and did not meaningfully reduce addiction rates. It did, however, reliably expand the government's power and budget, which tells you everything about why it persists.

Oregon ran the country's most ambitious drug policy experiment in 2020. Measure 110 decriminalized possession of small amounts of every controlled substance while leaving the supply chain fully criminal. Users faced only nominal consequences for possession but still got their product from cartels, at cartel prices, of cartel quality. In 2024 the legislature partially re-criminalized possession and declared the experiment a failure. The lesson Salem drew was that decriminalization doesn't work. The actual lesson was that half-measures fail, and that their failure is reliably used to justify re-expanding state power. The cartels were untouched throughout. They always are, until legalization takes away their monopoly.

The replacement's returns are in. In Multnomah County, where the crisis is most visible, more than a third of the people referred to deflection left before they were even assessed, only 18 percent ever followed up with a provider, and of those who entered the program, seven in ten never finished it. Participation did not reduce re-arrest rates.[^deflect] The supply chain, untouched, kept every customer.

Drug treatment should be private and voluntary. Forced treatment has a poor track record and violates bodily autonomy. Effective treatment exists in the market. Government should stop crowding it out and stop caging the people who need it.

## The State as Vice Merchant

**Position: Get the state out of the liquor business. A government that sells you whiskey, sets its price, and skims its own shelves has no claim to be regulating vice.**

Oregon sorts intoxicants into three tiers: drugs it prohibits, drugs it licenses and taxes, and one it sells itself. The OLCC (the same agency that licenses cannabis) controls the purchase, distribution, and pricing of every distilled spirit sold in Oregon, a Prohibition-era arrangement that persists because it generates revenue, not because anyone has explained why whiskey requires a state monopoly when beer and wine somehow do not. In 2023, the agency's executive director and five senior officials were caught diverting rare bourbon, including Pappy Van Winkle, from state inventory for their personal use. The director resigned, then negotiated a $500 ethics settlement; the commission rejected it as too lenient and imposed $1,500.[^olcc] The institution that decides which intoxicants Oregonians may buy, and at what price, was skimming its own inventory, and the penalty was less than the street value of a single bottle of the merchandise.

The monopoly is defended candidly, at least. In 2025 this district's own incumbent carried a bill to let grocery stores sell canned cocktails, written at the grocers' request and offered with a tax rate more than twice California's. It died. That November the commission voted to oppose privatization outright, having worked out that the state collects roughly $11.4 million on those products and would collect about $7.7 million if grocers sold them; its spirits director gave the reason plainly, and it was not public health. There is no new money in it. The 2026 successor bill died in committee too.[^hb3730] A monopoly that kills its own sponsor's concession twice and publishes the arithmetic has stopped bothering with a defense.

The bottle bill is an OLCC program too. In 1971 the state created a mandatory 10-cent deposit on beverage containers, an interest-free loan from every shopper, refundable upon performing a chore. The deposits you never claw back don't return to you: unclaimed refunds are kept by the Oregon Beverage Recycling Cooperative, the private distributor co-op that runs the system. Fifty years on, that dime had become the bearer currency of the street economy produced by the policies described above, and redemption centers had become hubs of the addiction crisis. The legislature's 2025 response was characteristic: not to end the program, but to restrict the hours at which you may redeem your own money.[^bottle] The state manufactured a currency, the currency funded a crisis the state also manufactured, and the remedy was a curfew on refunds.

The dime means nothing to the legislator who voted to restrict the hours you may redeem it, and everything to the person whose income is the dimes. Contempt for the poor, administered in ten-cent increments.

**Positions:**
- Abolish the OLCC's liquor monopoly. Privatize distribution and retail; let spirits be sold like beer, wine, and groceries — by businesses that can be fired by their customers.
- Repeal the bottle bill. Scrap aluminum has market value without legislation; where it doesn't, no statute makes recycling anything but theater. Oregonians can be trusted to dispose of a can without posting a bond first.

## Mental Health

**Position: Stop funding the system that produced the outcome. Oregon's mental health apparatus crowded out the churches, charities, and private operators who did this work, then billed taxpayers for the vacancy.**

Oregon ranks dead last — 51st — for adult mental illness prevalence.[^mha] The legislature's response has been to increase funding for the same systems that presided over that outcome. In any other enterprise this would be called doubling down on failure. In Salem it is called leadership.

The 2026 sequel made the mechanism visible: the behavioral-health benefit the state expanded overran its budget by nearly $300 million within months of that budget passing, and the remedy was to divert $170 million from the fund paying the primary care doctors, pediatricians, and OB-GYNs who serve the poor.[^oha] The objection is not that the state spent too little. The objection is that a monopoly without prices cannot budget. It overcommits, discovers the arithmetic late, and corrects at the expense of whichever of its dependents has the least political pull. Markets ration by price, continuously. Bureaus ration by committee, after the fact.

The standard progressive diagnosis of the visible crisis is that deinstitutionalization went too far, that Oregon should rebuild the state hospital capacity it lost through the late 20th century. This gets the history half right. Oregon did reduce institutional capacity. It also, at the same time, built a regulatory wall that prevented anyone else from replacing what was lost. Licensing requirements, scope-of-practice rules, facility regulations, and Certificate-of-Need laws made it illegal or prohibitively expensive for churches, voluntary associations, and private operators to do what the institutions had done — at lower cost, with actual consent, and without the worst abuses of the institutional era. The result was predictable. It is on every block.

**Positions:**
- Involuntary commitment as currently practiced in Oregon is preventive detention, caging people who have not committed an act of aggression based on a prediction that they might. Where someone *has* committed a violent act, restraint is justified. That is a question of justice, not mental health policy.
- Eliminate the government's mental health bureaucracy. The dollars absorbed by OHA overhead and state hospital operations would produce better outcomes in private hands, or simply left with the people who earned them. Oregon's mental health apparatus does not fail despite its funding. It fails commensurate with it.
- Remove state barriers to private mental health provision. Oregon's licensing and scope-of-practice rules restrict who may provide counseling, prescribe medication, or operate recovery facilities. These rules protect incumbent providers, not patients.
- Legalization of currently prohibited substances (see Drug Policy) is itself a mental health intervention. A significant share of people labeled mentally ill by government systems are self-medicating with criminalized substances. Removing that criminalization removes a major driver of crisis contacts, incarceration, and trauma.
- Mutual aid, peer support, and private recovery operate at a fraction of the cost of government programs and produce comparable or better outcomes for participants who choose them. Much of what is affordable is faith-based, because licensing walls keep secular alternatives from opening, not because recovery requires a church. Government should stop crowding all of it out with tax-funded competitors.

## Public Safety

**Position: Restitution over punishment, accountability over immunity. A justice system that answers to the victim needs neither a monopoly nor a cage to work.**

Portland's public safety situation deteriorated after 2020 because the city abandoned enforcement while keeping the same broken monopoly. The political debate has since moved to shuffling funds from the police bureau to Portland Street Response. Neither approach questions the monopoly itself.

Policing is a government monopoly with no competitive pressure to perform. When that monopoly refuses to enforce basic laws, people and businesses should be free to organize their own protection. The city has blocked those alternatives at every turn.

The "defund the police" movement of 2020 briefly approached a libertarian impulse and then veered away from it. Its failure was not radicalism but incompleteness: it tried to redirect funds from one government program to other government programs, rather than return them to the people who had been forced to fund failure in the first place. The alternative to a government monopoly on force is not a different government monopoly with a kinder name.

**Positions:**
- Remove legal barriers to private security districts, Business Improvement Districts with enforcement authority, and private arbitration of disputes. Government doesn't need to authorize these arrangements. It needs to stop claiming a monopoly on the functions they perform.
- Government should not be able to take your property without a criminal conviction. Civil asset forfeiture (seizing property without a conviction) is theft by the government, not law enforcement.
- Oregon has taken small steps on police accountability (a state-law cause of action for misconduct was created in 2021[^hb2204]) but never reformed qualified immunity itself. The unfinished work: abolish qualified immunity as a defense in state court, remove procedural barriers to suing officials personally, and end taxpayer indemnification of judgments against officers. An officer whose employer covers every judgment has no accountability — only insurance.
- The carceral system should be dismantled, not expanded. Incarceration is enormously expensive, corrupts those subjected to it, and serves the government's interests far more reliably than it serves victims'. Government has managed to build a system where the victim gets nothing, the taxpayer gets the bill, and the institution that failed to prevent the crime gets a bigger budget.
- Restitution, not punishment, should be the organizing principle of any justice system. The victim — not the government — is the injured party. This is the core insight of the restorative and transformative justice traditions that progressive reformers already champion; the libertarian disagreement is not about goals but about who should be trusted to deliver them. Private arbitration and contractual enforcement are how most human conflict was resolved before government monopolized the function and renamed it "justice."

## Firearms

**Position: No permission slip for a right. Every firearm restriction Oregon has on the table works the same way: the state defines who qualifies, then widens the definition.**

Oregon has seen a sustained push to restrict firearm ownership, most prominently Measure 114, a 2022 ballot initiative requiring a permit to purchase any firearm and banning magazines over ten rounds. It has never taken effect. The Court of Appeals upheld it in March 2025, the Oregon Supreme Court heard argument that November and has not ruled, and the legislature paused implementation into 2026. Four years after the vote, Oregonians still do not know whether it is law.[^m114]

Self-defense is a natural right. It exists whether a constitution recognizes it or not. The Second Amendment codifies that right but does not create it. A legislature that does not trust its constituents to own firearms has told you something important about its view of the relationship between citizen and government.

It is worth being direct about who bears the cost of disarmament. Minority communities, LGBTQ+ individuals, and others who have historically had the most reason to distrust state protection are also those who most need the ability to defend themselves. The police do not have a legal obligation to protect any individual; the Supreme Court has confirmed this repeatedly. And many of the same voices that argue police are systemically violent, unaccountable, and not to be trusted simultaneously argue that only police should be armed. If that critique of law enforcement is correct, then it is an argument *for* an armed citizenry, not against one.

**Positions:**
- Oppose Measure 114 and all permit-to-purchase schemes. Self-defense is not a privilege the state grants upon application — it is a natural right that precedes the state. Requiring prior approval to exercise it treats the citizen as a subject.

[Read more: Exposed Permit Data Shows Discretionary Systems Exclude Minorities and Women — Crime Prevention Research Center](https://crimeresearch.org/2017/07/concealed-handgun-permits-given-hispanics-blacks-women-los-angeles-county/)
- Oppose magazine capacity restrictions. They burden law-abiding owners without meaningfully affecting criminal use, and they establish the precedent that the government may define what a legal arm looks like.
- Oppose red flag laws as currently structured in Oregon. Confiscating firearms based on a unilateral ex parte order — without criminal charge, trial, or conviction — is a due process violation. The burden of proof must rest on the government, not on the gun owner to petition for their own property back.
- Oppose background checks. The check is not the objection. The file is.
- Support repeal of restrictions on carrying firearms. The right to bear arms is not limited to the home. Oregonians who lawfully own firearms should be able to carry them without government permission.
- Oppose firearms restrictions at every level of government: state, county, and municipal. A patchwork of local ordinances creates traps for law-abiding owners. The solution is fewer restrictions, not centralized permission from Salem.

A background check searches someone suspected of nothing, in advance, as the price of a right. Nobody is screened before they publish or worship, and not because speech is harmless.

Then look at who fails. Disqualification runs through categories the state writes (mental-health adjudication chief among them), and the state's own catalog of mental illness listed homosexuality until 1973.[^dsm] The institution now sorting who is too unwell to own a rifle was, within living memory, diagnosing the people with the most reason to want one. It would like to keep a record.

_Private sellers, ranges, and insurers may refuse anyone they please, and should. The question was never whether dangerous people can be screened out. It is who keeps the list._

## Personal Autonomy

**Position: The state is not a party to your risk. Once “society bears the cost” licenses the helmet law, nothing with a medical bill attached is out of reach.**

Oregon requires helmets for motorcyclists of every age and for bicyclists under 16.[^orshelmet] The risk from riding without one falls on the rider. It is not a public safety law in the sense that word is used elsewhere on this page: no one else's person or property is endangered by an uncovered head. It is a law that fines you for your own risk assessment.

The paternalist case is always the same one: society bears the cost of your injury, so society gets a vote on your choices. That argument has no stopping point. It justifies banning motorcycles outright, or skiing, or smoking, or a diet the state doesn't like. Anything with a healthcare cost falls under it. The honest fix for the externality, if the state insists one exists, is to let insurers price the risk, not to conscript the legislature into managing everyone's skull.

**Positions:**
- Repeal Oregon's mandatory motorcycle and bicycle helmet laws for adults. Competent adults may weigh risk to themselves without a permission slip from Salem.
- Oppose extending helmet, seatbelt-style, or similar self-risk mandates into new categories of activity. If the justification is "it might cost the system money," the state has justified regulating everything.
- Leave minors' safety equipment to parents, not statute, the same principle already applied to countless other child-rearing decisions the state does not micromanage.

## Education

**Position: Exit, not reform. Compulsory attendance, provider licensing, and vouchers alike keep the state's hand on the curriculum; only leaving costs it anything.**

Oregon's government schools produce mediocre outcomes at high cost. Portland's district in particular has struggled with poor reading and math proficiency, high administrative overhead, and a governance structure that answers to the teachers' union first and to families when it gets around to it.

The deeper problem is structural: **government education is a tax-funded monopoly delivered without meaningful competition.** The surprise is not that it performs poorly. The surprise is that the people who run it still expect to be believed.

And it is worth remembering what the monopoly was built to do. The American government school system was constructed in the mid-19th century by reformers who stated their goals openly: cultural assimilation of Catholic immigrants, labor discipline for the emerging industrial economy, and the erasure of languages and customs the reformers considered unsuitable for American life. The institution performs its original purposes better than it performs the ones its modern defenders assign it. That is not a flaw. It is the design working as intended.

The families with the least still get the worst of it: assigned schools, no exit, and a union negotiating against them with their own taxes. The people progressives speak for are the ones this monopoly eats first.

**Positions:**
- Support full educational freedom: repeal compulsory attendance laws and allow families to choose any educational arrangement — homeschooling, private school, microschools, apprenticeships — without government interference or required reporting. These are not theoretical alternatives; they exist, they work, and they expand rapidly wherever government does not suppress them.
- Remove state barriers to new educational providers. Certificate-of-need requirements, accreditation mandates, and facility regulations function as incumbent-protection devices, not quality controls.

[Read more: Education: Free and Compulsory — Murray N. Rothbard, Mises Institute](https://mises.org/library/book/education-free-and-compulsory)
- Oppose voucher programs. Vouchers do not liberate education; they extend the government's franchise into private institutions. Any private school accepting government money will, predictably and inevitably, be subjected to government curriculum, hiring, and admissions standards. The history of every regulated industry confirms this. The goal is separation of school and state, not a new funding pipeline with strings attached.

Libertarians have good-faith disagreements on vouchers. The concern here is simple: every industry that accepted government funding on "neutral" terms eventually accepted government control of its operations. Education will not be the exception. It never is.
- The position is the elimination of government-funded education. We do not run government grocery stores. The argument for why education is different has never been made, only assumed, by the people whose salaries depend on the assumption.
- Repeal occupational licensing requirements for teachers. There is no credible evidence that licensure improves student outcomes. It restricts entry, raises costs, and protects incumbents.
- Oppose centralized curriculum mandates and standardized testing regimes that homogenize instruction and remove teacher and family autonomy.

## Removing Barriers to Work

**Position: Let people prove themselves without asking first. Oregon gates 191 occupations, hair braiding and floor sanding among them, and a credential that informs a customer does the same work without the ban.**

Oregon restricts entry into the labor market from both ends: it dictates who may work through occupational licensing, and what they may be paid through the minimum wage. Both harm the people they claim to protect. This is not a side effect. It is the mechanism. The people the law is named after are the people the law hurts.

**Positions:**
- Repeal occupational licensing statutes systematically. The presumption should be against licensure; the burden of proof rests entirely on those who want to prohibit competition. Oregon licenses 191 of the 331 occupations surveyed by the State Occupational Licensing Index[^soli], with no credible evidence that licensure improves consumer outcomes in most of them. Sanding a floor commercially requires four years of training.[^floor] This is not a joke.
- Where information asymmetries exist, voluntary certification (credentialing that signals quality without prohibiting unlicensed competitors) is the market's own solution. Consumers are capable of evaluating credentials and reading reviews. Government doesn't need to be involved.

[Read more: Occupational Licensing in Oregon — Institute for Justice](https://ij.org/issues/economic-liberty/occupational-licensing/oregon/)

- Repeal the state minimum wage. It does not increase the total compensation available in the economy; it reallocates it toward workers who keep their jobs and away from those who lose theirs, and the workers most likely to be priced out are the ones the law is named after.

[Read more: Employment Effects of Minimum Wages — IZA World of Labor](https://wol.iza.org/articles/employment-effects-of-minimum-wages/long)

- Repeal state and local restrictions on gig work, independent contracting, and flexible employment. Laws like AB5-style reclassification mandates destroy the work arrangements that many workers — particularly part-time workers, caregivers, and people building skills — deliberately choose.

_The minimum wage has a history its current defenders rarely discuss. It was championed in the early 20th century by Progressive Era reformers and labor economists who said openly that its purpose was to price "unemployable" workers — women, Black Americans, immigrants, the disabled — out of the labor market. This is not a libertarian reinterpretation. It is in the published work of the people who built the policy. The mechanism has not changed. The euphemism has. The workers the law was designed to exclude are still the workers it excludes most reliably, and its defenders have simply stopped saying so out loud._

[Read more: Eugenics and Economics in the Progressive Era — Thomas C. Leonard, Journal of Economic Perspectives (PDF)](https://www.princeton.edu/~tleonard/papers/retrospectives.pdf)

## Taxation

**Position: The money is not the state's to begin with. Oregon taxes what you earn, what a company takes in whether or not it profits, and the house you finished paying for, and will only argue about the percentages.**

Oregon has the highest effective income tax rate in the country for both single and joint filers. Portland adds the highest local income tax rate of any city in the nation. Oregon's top state rate of 9.9% kicks in at $125,000, a working professional's income in Portland, not a wealthy one.[^tax] When businesses can't afford to grow, the workers who don't get hired pay the price too. It also imposes corporate taxes, the Corporate Activity Tax (a gross receipts tax that taxes revenue even when businesses lose money), payroll taxes, and a dense lattice of local levies and fees.

[Read more: State Individual Income Tax Rates — Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/state/state-income-tax-rates/)

**Taxation is not a contribution. It is the extraction of money under threat of force from people who have harmed no one, by an institution that has never been required to demonstrate it spent the last extraction well before demanding the next one.**

The practical case against it is equally strong: every dollar collected and redistributed through government passes through a system with administrative overhead, political distortion, and no feedback mechanism. The alternative is not chaos — it is individuals, families, businesses, and voluntary associations deploying their own resources according to their own judgment. Government calls this "lost revenue." Everyone else calls it "keeping what you earned." No other institution gets to describe your paycheck as its loss.

The poor in this district are not under-taxed. They are over-licensed, over-policed, and over-zoned. Salem's idea of solidarity is another bureau.

**Positions:**
- Oppose all tax increases, categorically.
- Repeal the Corporate Activity Tax. A gross receipts tax is economically illiterate: it taxes companies that are losing money and distorts business decisions in ways that harm workers and consumers.
- Support repeal of the state income tax. Oregon's reliance on income tax makes state revenues extremely volatile and punishes the one activity government should be least interested in discouraging: productive work.
- Tax expenditures (credits and deductions) are preferable to equivalent spending programs — paying less tax is better than paying more and getting some back — but they obscure the true cost of government and allocate investment based on political favoritism rather than market signals.
- Property tax is a claim by the government that you occupy your home at its pleasure, rent paid to the state for the privilege of not being evicted from what you already own. Oregon's Measures 5 and 50 layer arbitrary inequities on top of that, but the design flaws are not the objection. The tax itself is. Abolish it.

## Public Transportation

**Position: Let riders pay for rides. Fares cover 8.5% of TriMet's operating cost; the other 91.5% comes from people who never board.**

TriMet is treated in Oregon political culture as a public good beyond scrutiny. It is not. It is a subsidized government monopoly that imposes real costs on non-riders, cannot cover its own operating expenses, and is structurally insulated from the market feedback that would force any private operator to improve or exit.

**The fiscal reality:** TriMet's farebox revenue covers only about 8.5% of operating costs as of FY2025, down from nearly 40% a decade ago.[^farebox] There is no plausible path to self-sufficiency, and none is seriously proposed. Your neighborhood lemonade stand has better unit economics.

**Buses** damage pavement at rates that rise with roughly the fourth power of axle weight, standard pavement engineering. A loaded 40-foot bus puts roughly ten times a car's weight on each axle, which is ten thousand times the damage from a single vehicle. More conservative assumptions put it nearer 1,200, a figure KGW's Verify desk examined and found sound. Either number is a subsidy paid in asphalt by people who never board.[^pavement] TriMet's battery-electric buses carry several tons of battery on the same two axles. Routes operate well below capacity for much of the day, concentrating pavement destruction on corridors paid for by all taxpayers. Buses stop in travel lanes without pullouts, creating arterial congestion borne by every driver on those corridors.

**MAX light rail** is worse. Tracks permanently consume traffic lanes, grade crossings halt traffic in every direction each time a train passes, and the signal preemption system confuses drivers navigating unfamiliar intersections. MAX extensions have been built using eminent domain: TriMet condemned over 140 properties for the Division Transit Project alone[^division] and routinely offered owners a fraction of fair value, forcing them to litigate. In one Milwaukie line case, a jury returned a verdict six times TriMet's original offer.[^walnut]

The Interstate Bridge Replacement carries the pattern to its conclusion. A 1.9-mile MAX extension into Vancouver sits inside a program whose estimate rose from $6 billion in 2022 to $14.4 billion in March 2026. The response to that number was to fund a first phase that designs the transit crossing without laying any track: no service before 2036, and a federal grant decision that will not arrive until 2030. Vancouver's mayor, who wants the extension built, put the problem to her own side plainly: “All of the opponents get it. They can say they're building a light rail to nowhere.”[^ibr] This is not infrastructure. It is a monument to the political class that funds it.

**Positions:**
- Oppose all operating subsidies for TriMet and any expansion of the transit network funded by government debt or taxation.
- Support opening transit corridors to private operators. If routes are genuinely valuable, private providers can serve them without a captive tax base. Portland's regulatory framework effectively prohibits jitneys and microtransit, protections that exist to insulate TriMet from competition, not to serve riders.
- TriMet should be privatized or dissolved. Transit does not require government provision any more than freight shipping or air travel.

## Environment & Energy

**Position: Sue the polluter, don't license him. A polluter who fouls your land, your water, or your lungs owes you compensation; an agency that sells him a permit owes you nothing.**

Environmental protection in Oregon is administered the way the state administers everything else described on this page: as a permission market run by agencies, paid for by everyone, and accountable to no one in particular. The principled alternative is older and stricter — property rights. Pollution that damages a specific person's land, water, air, or health is a trespass, and the polluter owes the victim. A court adjudicating a trespass cannot grant permission to harm. An agency can — that is its core product. Regulation converts pollution into a licensing question: pay the fee, hold the permit, and the harm becomes legal.

The Climate Protection Program is the case study. When the legislature declined to pass cap-and-trade, the program was enacted by executive order instead. It exempts 36 of the state's largest natural gas users from compliance until 2028, routes a projected $150 million a year to nonprofits outside voter accountability, and was promulgated without an estimate of its cost to Oregonians.[^cpp] It is being challenged in court by a coalition of manufacturers, utility suppliers — and labor unions. UA Local 290, the plumbers and pipefitters, put it plainly: the program "harms every consumer and every layer of business in Oregon."[^ua290] When the union and the manufacturer file on the same side, attention should be paid.

Cap-and-trade does not make anyone whole. It creates a market in government permissions, and like every permission market, it is dominated by whoever can afford the permissions, exempts whoever has the lobbyists, and bills whoever has neither.

**Positions:**
- Repeal the Climate Protection Program. A rule with no cost estimate, enacted without the legislature, is not climate policy. It is administrative fiat.
- Oppose cap-and-trade and carbon pricing at every level of government.
- Repeal renewable energy mandates. Mandates raise electricity rates, among the most regressive costs government can impose, while insulating utilities from accountability for the choice.
- Restore tort and trespass remedies for environmental harm. A polluter who damages identifiable people or property should compensate them fully, in court, without a permit as a shield.
- End energy subsidies of every kind, fossil and renewable alike. The state should not be picking energy sources any more than grocery brands.
- End the state's practice of exempting itself and its favored operators from the standards it imposes on everyone else.

## Immigration

**Position: The border is not on the ballot here. Salem decides whether Oregon lends its jails and its data to federal enforcement, and whether an immigrant who already has a trade is allowed to practice it.**

A person does not lose the right to work, trade, or rent a home by being born on the wrong side of a line. Free movement of labor follows from the same premises as every other position here: self-ownership and voluntary exchange. An agreement between a willing worker and a willing employer is not the federal government's to approve, and it is certainly not Salem's to police on the federal government's behalf.

A state legislator casts no vote on border policy. What a state legislator votes on is whether Oregon participates: whether state and local personnel, data, and jail space are lent to federal enforcement. They should not be. And the most binding immigration controls in Oregon are not federal at all. They are the state's own licensing walls. The occupational licensing regime described under Removing Barriers to Work falls hardest on immigrants, for whom a trade learned over a lifetime is illegal to practice without years of incumbent-designed credentialing. A state that wanted immigrants to prosper would not need a new program. It would need to delete the ones standing in their way.

**Positions:**
- Maintain and strengthen Oregon's sanctuary status: no state or local personnel, data, or jail space for federal immigration enforcement.
- Oppose any state E-Verify mandate. Requiring federal permission before taking a job treats the right to work as a government grant.
- Repeal the licensing barriers that lock immigrants out of legal work (see Removing Barriers to Work).
- Support free movement of labor. A path to citizenship is a second-best remedy that presumes the legitimacy of the underlying controls; relief for people already living and working here should not wait on that presumption.

## Abortion

**Position: The same power forbids and compels. A legislature trusted to prohibit a medical decision is the one that will later require one; keep it out of the room and off the invoice.**

This section is short because the position is that the legislature has no business here — from either direction.

Self-ownership settles it. What a person does with their own body is not a legislative question, and a state empowered to prohibit a medical decision is the same state empowered to mandate one. I oppose legal restrictions on abortion. I also oppose taxpayer funding of it: Oregon currently funds abortion through state programs, which forces people with sincere moral objections to pay for what they consider a grave wrong. The two major parties offer the two flavors of state involvement: one would use government power to prohibit, the other uses it to subsidize. The consistent position, the one neither party can offer, is that the state stays out: not as prohibitor, not as paymaster.

**Positions:**
- Oppose all legal restrictions on abortion.
- Oppose taxpayer funding of abortion, as of any medical procedure; repeal state-funded coverage mandates.
- Neutrality is the position: no prohibition, no subsidy, no role.

## What This Campaign Is Not

This platform does not promise that libertarian policies will solve every problem painlessly or immediately. Markets produce better outcomes than political allocation over time, but markets also reveal real scarcities that subsidies and mandates have been masking. Some of those revelations will be uncomfortable. That is what honesty costs.

What this campaign does promise is that you will not be lied to. Government makes the problems it claims to solve worse. The people of District 42 are capable of running their own lives without a legislature's supervision. Every election, candidates from both parties tell you they'll fight for you, meaning they'll spend your money, expand their authority, and never once ask whether you wanted the help.

Government is not your guardian, your parent, or your salvation. It is an institution that takes money under threat of imprisonment from people who have harmed no one and distributes it according to political incentive.

### On Not Winning

District 42 is one of the safest Democratic seats in Oregon, and a win here would put one Libertarian in a chamber of sixty. Both are true.

A legislature runs less on votes than on what its members can be relied upon not to say. A member who needs no caucus, no endorsement and no donor can describe a bill accurately on the floor while it is still a bill. Salem's worst work is not done over objections. It is done in their absence.

The rest is the number. Both parties read returns, and an unopposed incumbent has been told the record is satisfactory. A serious share of the ballots going to someone who wants the office abolished says something they cannot file away, and it is the only part of this campaign the legislature is obliged to read.
